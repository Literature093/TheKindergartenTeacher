A Literary Exploration of Education, Dreams, and Society

Introduction to Aung Thinn

Aung Thinn, a respected literary figure from Myanmar, is widely recognized for his insightful explorations of education and social issues. His works are deeply rooted in personal experiences and the historical realities of his country, allowing readers to engage with narratives that are both authentic and thought-provoking.

Significance in Myanmar Literature

Aung Thinn has made significant contributions to contemporary Myanmar literature, particularly in the field of educational storytelling. His works highlight the importance of teaching and learning as transformative forces within society.

"Education is not just learning—it is transformation."

The Kindergarten Teacher

Part 1

I was a lecturer at the University of Yangon since the early 1960s. Before that, I served about three years as a middle school teacher in my hometown of Taungdwin-gyi. At the risk of being thought boastful, I must say that I was considered quite a good teacher at both posts. Actually, I had thought it was nothing much: one becomes known as a "good teacher" without too much effort or talent. However, what I encountered on my trip home in 1963 shook me. I arrived just as the schools reopened for the new semester, and I realized I had been way off the mark with my evaluation of "good teaching."

Part 2

Let me explain. On that visit to Taungdwin-gyi, I liked to spend my time at the Shwe In Taung Pagoda, where a little museum had been set up. I was one of the directors of the museum along with some of my best friends, so we liked to meet there in the evenings. On my way to the museum, I would drop in at the primary school next to the pagoda to inform my friend, U Nyan Sein, that I'd be waiting for him. He taught kindergarten.

Part 3

One day I stood at the door of his class while he was drawing on the blackboard. His class of five-year-olds waited silently. After a few strokes, they recognized the drawing. "It's Master Tortoise!" they cried. He added details. "He's holding a walking stick!" "He's smoking a pipe!" Then he began telling a story as he drew.

Listen to Part 3

Part 4

For several days, I saw him doing the same thing—drawing and storytelling. So I asked him why he wasn't teaching lessons. "I can teach reading and writing anytime," he said. "Right now, it's important that children enjoy school. They must love coming to school, not fear it." His answer made me think.

Part 5

One day, a young boy sat crying in the front row. He ignored everything and kept looking out the window at an elderly woman sitting under a tamarind tree. U Nyan Sein called out to her to move away so the boy would stop crying. When she did, the boy cried even louder. "This is awful," he told me.

Listen to Part 5

Part 6

Then he smiled and said to the class, "It seems this boy has too much crying left. Why don't you all help him use it up?" The children began pretending to cry loudly. The boy stopped in surprise. Soon, everyone stopped crying—and the boy calmed down.

Part 7

The next day, he announced, "I'm going to give you some plums!" The children got excited. He drew plums on the board and pretended to throw them. The children pretended to eat them—some sweet, some sour, some rotten—laughing the whole time.

Listen to Part 7

Part 8

The following day, he said he would "buy" plums from them. He showed them how to draw a "sweet plum" (a proper circle) and a "rotten plum" (drawn the wrong way). The children copied the movements and even corrected each other.

Listen to Part 8

Part 9

The children drew plums on their slates and showed them to him. He praised good ones and gently corrected bad ones. Even the boy who used to cry became eager and happy, proudly showing his work again and again.

Listen to Part 9

Part 10

The next lesson introduced letters. He compared each letter to having a name, just like the children. The class continued learning joyfully. Watching all this, I was deeply shaken. Was I really a good teacher? Was I doing as much for my students as he was?
